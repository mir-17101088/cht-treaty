The Accord ended an insurgency. The killing went on.

Fifty years in the Chittagong Hill Tracts, from a drowned valley to a peace deal to the gunfire that never quite stopped, told through the violence that was recorded.

By Mir Rownak

On 2 December 1997, Abul Hasnat Abdullah for the government and Santu Larma for the Parbatya Chattagram Jana Samhati Samiti put their names to a peace accord for the Chittagong Hill Tracts. It closed about two decades of armed struggle in the hills.

The hills are three districts, Rangamati, Khagrachhari and Bandarban, about a tenth of Bangladesh's land. Before 1971, almost everyone who lived there belonged to one of 13 indigenous peoples.

Nearly 29 years after the signing, people in those hills are still being killed. This is the story of what changed when the insurgency ended, and what did not, told through the violence that was written down.

To tell it, The Daily Star compiled a dossier of more than 1,200 violent incidents in the three districts over the past 50 years, from 1976 to November 2026. It draws on verifiable sources: researchers in Sweden who track the world's armed conflicts, a study of political violence in the newspapers, a global monitor of political violence and protest, and reports by human rights investigators, commissions and journalists. This story is our analysis of that dossier.

The analysis shows three things. The big massacres all but stopped. The killing did not. And over the past decade it has moved south, into Bandarban.

The drowned valley

The trouble, by many accounts, began with water.

Between 1957 and 1963 a dam was built at Kaptai. Its lake swallowed more than 400 square miles, including at least 54,000 acres of settled farmland, most of it Chakma.

Some 100,000 people lost their homes and land. Compensation, human rights investigators later wrote, was inadequate, and more than 40,000 Chakma crossed into India.

Mohendro Chakma's family home went under the lake; years of moving between camps followed. Nibedita Chakma was ten when her family left Boradam village. One village elder remembers leaving belongings behind and passing through 18 camps.

In 1963, the year most accounts give for the dam's completion, the hills also lost the special status they had held under Pakistan's constitution.

After independence, in February 1972, a delegation from the hills took four demands to Sheikh Mujibur Rahman: autonomy, a legislature of their own, the old regulation that protected the region, and a ban on outsiders settling there. All four were refused.

That October, as the Constituent Assembly debated the new constitution, a member from the hills, Manabendra Narayan Larma, objected to every citizen being called "Bangalee". When the clause passed, he walked out.

The law minister replied that the constitution gave every citizen equal status. An army brigade commander, writing years later, put the state's view more plainly: autonomy was turned down "on account of greater national unity".

Larma's party, the Parbatya Chattagram Jana Samhati Samiti, or PCJSS, grew an armed wing, the Shanti Bahini. By the middle of the 1970s it was fighting.

The insurgency years

Then came the settlers. From the late 1970s, Bengali families from the plains were brought into the hills; an army officer describes them as landless families given five acres each.

How many came is still argued over: over 40,000 by one count from 1997, nearly 400,000 between 1979 and 1985 by others. What is not in doubt is the change. Non-indigenous people were about 11.5 percent of the hills in 1974, almost 34 percent by 1980, and near parity by the middle of that decade.

The army reportedly armed some of the new settlers as Village Defence Parties. Human rights investigators wrote that settlers began attacking hill villages from 1980, often alongside army operations. The Shanti Bahini attacked settlers too.

In March 1980 at Kawkhali, near Rangamati, the Shanti Bahini reportedly ambushed soldiers where Bengali families were being resettled. The army's reprisal, human rights investigators wrote, fell on unarmed villagers who had been ordered to line up. Hill sources put the dead at 50 to more than 200; no official toll is known.

On 31 May 1984 an armed group attacked the Bengali settlement at Bhushanchhara in Barkal. Human rights investigators said Shanti Bahini units killed at least 77 settlers; local accounts say about 400. Nurnahar Begum, a survivor, says 13 members of the family died. A former Shanti Bahini commander named in that account calls the accusation propaganda.

The next day, hill families in six mouzas, or revenue villages, of Barkal were reportedly attacked by army forces, and at least 110 villagers were said to have been killed. The domestic press carried nothing about it.

By early 1987, tens of thousands had fled across the border to India: about 40,000 by Indian estimates, about 25,000 by Bangladesh's.

For these years there is no systematic count. What exists are accounts written afterwards by commissions, rights groups and the PCJSS itself.

Gathered together, they describe 19 attacks before 1989 that each killed ten people or more; the 17 that give a toll add up to ~2,600 to ~2,900 deaths.

Nine of the 19, holding about half of those deaths, are known only from one side's account. They are the massacres people remember, not a full count of the dead.

Official figures later put the toll of two decades at more than 8,500; a 1997 report said at least 20,000. Nobody knows.

Cluster villages

From 1989 there is a year-by-year count, compiled by the conflict researchers from news coverage, and the insurgency comes into focus. Between the start of 1989 and the Accord, they counted 113 deadly incidents in the hills, about 13 a year. At least 550 people died in them, and perhaps as many as 742.

Almost all of it was the insurgency. In 55 of those incidents, security forces fought a hill armed group; in 53, civilians were killed in attacks attributed to the Shanti Bahini. At the time, human rights investigators noted that newspapers regularly carried accounts of Shanti Bahini abuses while allegations against the security forces rarely appeared.

Villagers were caught between both. Hill families from scattered hamlets were moved into "cluster villages" built near army camps, meant to cut the Shanti Bahini's supply lines.

When district council elections were held in June 1989, the Shanti Bahini ordered a boycott and soldiers ordered people to vote. One woman from a cluster village near Khagrachhari said soldiers marched villagers to the polling booth at gunpoint.

Earlier that year, the Shanti Bahini was blamed for killing 13 non-tribal people near Kaptai. In May, at Langadu, at least 36 hill villagers were reportedly killed by Village Defence Party members and settlers after the council's non-tribal chairman was killed, allegedly by the Shanti Bahini. An official inquiry said 16 died at the hands of an "unruly mob". Thousands crossed into Tripura.

In August, a bomb on a passenger boat at Naniarchar killed 12 people, eight of them from the hill peoples; the attack was attributed to the Shanti Bahini.

Then came Logang.

Logang, in Panchhari, was a cluster village of about 600 houses, with a Bangladesh Rifles camp 250 yards away. On 10 April 1992 a young Bengali died; accounts differ on how. Around noon, settlers, Village Defence Party members and Ansars, an auxiliary force, attacked.

People were reportedly forced into their homes, which were set alight, and those who ran were shot. A 17-year-old said he saw three children thrown into the fire. The next day the district council's chairman, from the hill peoples, saw 138 bodies laid out for cremation.

Officials spoke of 11 to 13 dead, and the army first blamed a Shanti Bahini attack; the government later admitted that paramilitary forces acting with Bengali civilians had killed 12, and eight people were charged. A visiting group that included the deputy attorney general reported more than 200. Hill organisations still say more than 1,000. At a donors' meeting in Paris, the finance minister called the charges "a contrived situation to embarrass my delegation here".

In the recorded data, Logang is the deadliest single incident since 1989: at least 138 dead, and up to 300. For 1992, the conflict researchers counted 215 to 377 deaths, the most of any year they cover.

Four months later, in August 1992, the PCJSS declared a ceasefire. Killing went on. On 17 November 1993 at Naniarchar, hill students marched against an army check-post set up in a passenger shelter by the lake.

What followed depends on who tells it. Tribal witnesses said soldiers opened fire without warning, and that settlers attacked people who jumped into Kaptai lake. Human rights investigators held a list of 29 dead, among them a 70-year-old woman and a Buddhist monk.

The government first said 13 died, then 20. Later it said at least 15 were killed in a clash between rival student groups, that police fired in self-defence, and that many died after jumping into the lake.

Across the nearly nine years from 1989 to the Accord, our analysis of the recorded data finds eight incidents that each killed ten or more people, 257 to 419 lives in all.

Logang is one.

Five involved the Shanti Bahini: four attacks on civilians attributed to it, the largest killing 30 people in Rangamati in September 1996, by the military's account, and one clash with security forces.

Two were killings by Myanmar government forces on the border.

The insurgency's last years were quieter. Human rights investigators noted far fewer reported abuses in 1996 and 1997. Refugees in Tripura had begun coming home in 1994, but at the signing tens of thousands were still there; by 2000 all of the roughly 64,000 had returned, a substantial number of them, it was believed, without land.

One case from those years is still cited as proof of impunity. In June 1996, Kalpana Chakma, a 23-year-old organiser of the Hill Women's Federation, was taken from her home in Baghaichhari, blindfolded, with two of her brothers, who escaped under fire. Her family names an army officer and two Village Defence Party members. No one has been held to account.

She was a first-year college student, the only one in her family to study. In 2016 the case's 39th investigating officer filed a final report. In April 2024 a court in Rangamati accepted it and absolved all suspects.

The Accord

Talks had run for twelve years and through three governments: six rounds under Ershad, 13 under Khaleda Zia, seven under Sheikh Hasina.

The Accord promised

  • a regional council over the three district councils, two-thirds of its seats for hill people;
  • a land commission under a retired judge, able to cancel illegal settlements;
  • the phased withdrawal of temporary army camps;
  • the rehabilitation of refugees and the displaced;
  • and amnesty for Shanti Bahini fighters.

By March 1998 about 2,000 fighters had handed in their weapons.

Not everyone celebrated. The BNP-led opposition called three national strikes and named the deal a "black treaty"; Khaleda Zia said it made settlers "second class citizens". In the hills, the Hill Students Council, the Hill People's Council and the Hill Women's Federation said it fell short on autonomy, land and the army's withdrawal.

Peace, of a kind

The fighting had already slowed after the PCJSS's ceasefire of August 1992. After the signing, it all but stopped: the conflict researchers had counted 113 deadly incidents in the hills in the nine years before the Accord.

In the twelve years after, they counted four.

The massacres stopped too. Only one recorded incident after the Accord killed ten people or more: fighting on the Myanmar border between Myanmar government forces and a Rohingya armed group, in which 11 died in November 2000.

A single source, UCDP, reports it, placing it in Bandarban. Since then, no recorded incident has killed ten or more.

But the killing did not stop. Deadly incidents, most of them small, have been recorded in almost every year since the Accord.

Much of what was promised moved slowly. By the middle of 1999 the land commission had not met once, and of several hundred temporary army camps only 32 had been lifted.

Brother against brother

Much of the fighting that came next was between hill parties. On 26 December 1998, at a conference called by the same three councils, activists who said they would keep fighting for full autonomy launched the United People's Democratic Front, the UPDF, with Prasit Bikash Khisa among its leaders. Its manifesto spoke of "the political bankruptcy of the PCJSS".

In March 1998 police arrested four students in Dighinala and, human rights investigators said, beat them severely; the dissident groups said the PCJSS had helped get their members arrested. On 12 December 1999, Manin Chakma, 37, a former Shanti Bahini fighter who had given up his gun under the Accord, was shot dead at home; the PCJSS said he was the fifth victim since the signing of attacks it blamed on Prasit Bikash Khisa's group. The dissidents counted their own dead, among them Kusum Priyo Chakma and Pradip Lal Chakma, stabbed to death on their way home from a meeting in April 1998. By 2000 a commission had counted some 40 activists killed, each side calling the other the aggressor.

Many of the dead were young. The commission found that many activists on both sides were teenagers or school drop-outs, and some villages had become too dangerous for either side to visit.

The newspaper study covers political violence of any size from 1990 to 2018. Of its 14 deadly incidents before the Accord, none was between hill parties. In the twelve years after, 26 of its 46 were.

Fighting between the state and an armed group went the other way. Taking the conflict researchers and the newspaper study together, the same two sources on both sides of the Accord, it fell from 49 of 102 deadly incidents, about half, to 5 of 50, one in ten.

Each deadly incident also grew smaller. Before the Accord, a deadly incident in the newspaper study took 3.4 lives on average. After it, 2.0. Between 2010 and 2018, 1.5. After the Accord, most deadly incidents took one or two lives, like the shooting of Manin Chakma at his home.

The parties kept splitting. A breakaway PCJSS, known by the name of the party's founder, MN Larma, formed during the 2007–08 caretaker years. In November 2017 a group broke from the UPDF and called itself UPDF (Democratic).

In the six months that followed, this newspaper counted at least 17 people killed in Khagrachhari and Rangamati, most of them party members. On 3 May 2018, gunmen fired on a microbus carrying activists to the funeral of Shaktiman Chakma, the Naniarchar upazila chairman killed the day before. Five died, among them Tapan Jyoti Chakma, president of UPDF (Democratic).

Shaktiman Chakma's fellow activists blamed the UPDF, which denied it; UPDF (Democratic) blamed a feud inside the UPDF.

That year the killing also reached people outside the parties. On 3 January, Mithun Chakma, of the UPDF's media wing, was abducted near home in Khagrachhari town and shot; an aunt noted it was his two-year-old son's birthday. On 18 August, gunmen attacked a market in the town and, hours later, a UPDF procession nearby: seven died that day, three UPDF men and four bystanders, among them a health worker, a textile engineer, a student and a farmer.

Of all these rivalries, one runs longest. From the Accord to November 2026, the recorded data show 56 deadly incidents between the PCJSS and the UPDF, far more than any other pair.

Land and fire

Alongside the gunfire ran another conflict, older and harder to count: who owns the land. Again and again, a single killing or abduction set off attacks on whole villages.

In August 2003, after a Bengali businessman was abducted at Mahalchhari, houses in nearby hill villages were set alight. Officials listed 177 burnt houses in three villages; hill residents said about 350. A week later more than 1,500 people were still sheltering in the open and in the forest. "We had no other way but to flee to the jungles," one villager said.

At Sajek in April 2008, houses burned in seven villages in a single night. Hill residents said 77 of their houses burned and 15 settler huts were destroyed; settlers said it was the other way round.

At Baghaichhari in February 2010, human rights investigators reported, settlers set fire to at least 40 houses of hill families; the next day, after a demonstrator reportedly knifed an army commander, the army fired on demonstrators. Officials confirmed two dead; local people said at least six more died and that their bodies had not been recovered. In Khagrachhari the next year, three settlers were allegedly killed after a dispute over planting on ancestral land, and at least 60 hill homes were burned.

In June 2017, after a Bengali man's body was found, crowds attacked three Chakma villages at Langadu. Gunamala Chakma, an old woman who could not hear, was reported burned to death; police later said villagers had given them bones they believed were hers, and sent them for forensic tests.

The police said their 40 officers could not control a crowd of 7,000 to 8,000. The local army commander said troops stood between the two sides and averted a clash.

The Accord's answer to all this was meant to be the land commission. By 2020 it had more than 22,000 applications before it and had not resolved a single one.

Even the Accord's progress is disputed. The Awami League government said 48, and later 65, of its 72 sections were implemented; the PCJSS says about 25. "The accord is a contract, not a concession," the writer Milinda Marma argued in this newspaper.

South, to Bandarban

For the past decade and a half there is a third source: ACLED, which logs political violence and protest of every size, with no minimum death toll. Measured against its own earlier years, the violence it recorded grew, though news coverage may have changed too. From 2010 to 2018 it logged about 12 deadly incidents a year. From 2019 to May 2025, about 23, nearly double.

The violence also moved. Bandarban, the southernmost district, held 9 of the 104 deadly incidents ACLED logged in 2010–2018,

and 55 of 148 from 2019 to May 2025.

ACLED places 19 deadly incidents in or near Ruma upazila over those years, and 13 in or near Rowangchhari.

Part of the story has a name. A new armed group, the Kuki-Chin National Front, emerged in 2022, reportedly from the Bawm community, demanding a separate state across nine upazilas. In two years it was credited with at least nine large attacks.

In April 2023, eight people were killed in a shootout at Khamtang Para in Rowangchhari, which police said was between the UPDF and the group's armed wing. Locals said all eight were Bawm. More than 400 villagers fled to schools and offices. Sang Kung Bawam, whose husband was among the dead, said he was in no gang. "How will we survive?" she asked.

In April 2024 armed men stormed the upazila complex in Ruma, took 14 weapons and abducted a bank manager, then robbed two banks in Thanchi. The army's operation that followed led, human rights investigators said, to the arbitrary arrest of more than 100 Bawm people; many others fled to the jungle or across the border.

When some families came home in 2025, Damun Bawm, 70, who had taken six days to reach Mizoram, found the house in ruins. "I couldn't even step into my old home," Damun Bawm said.

Bandarban also borders Myanmar, and that conflict spills over. In February 2024, mortar shells and gunfire from fighting across the border fell on Ghumdhum in Naikhongchhari. One shell hit the house of Hosneara Khatun, 45, killing her and a Rohingya labourer. "We suffered a lot. The children are traumatised," said Mohammad Nabi of Tumbru.

The fighting itself changed character. In a period two and a half years shorter, the armed clashes ACLED logged rose from 33 to 63, bombings and other explosions from 5 to 21, and abductions from 5 to 13. Deadly fighting between the state and an armed group rose from 2 incidents to 22. Violent protests fell, from 62 to 35.

In the years ACLED covers, at least 339 people were injured and at least 55 abducted. Those are only the people its reports put a number on.

Still no peace

In September 2024, violence flared in Khagrachhari and Rangamati. Four people were killed, among them Dhananjoy Chakma, 50, Rubel Tripura, 25, and Junan Chakma, 20, and at least 72 were injured. In Dighinala, the local administration said 102 shops burned; in Rangamati, the office of the regional council the Accord created was set on fire.

The accounts split again. A Bengali marcher said the march was peaceful and was blocked; a hill resident said nobody stopped it and homes were burned unprovoked. The army said soldiers fired in self-defence after UPDF members shot at a patrol.

A year later, on 28 September 2025, at Guimara, three young men were shot dead during a blockade over the alleged gang rape of a girl: Athui Marma, 21, Athrau Marma, 22, and Teiching Marma, 20. Witnesses said law enforcers opened fire; the police said it was not known who fired; the army said armed UPDF members fired on crowds and troops, which the UPDF disputed.

Mibu Marma lost a home and a shop.

Since June 2025, the recorded data are less complete, because ACLED's coverage ends in May 2025. Even so, they show 24 deadly incidents and 32 to 35 deaths to the end of October 2026, eight of them between hill parties. A smaller count is not a sign of calm.

The killing goes on. In April 2026, Dharma Sing Chakma of the Democratic Youth Forum was shot dead in Rangamati Sadar, and two sisters were wounded; the UPDF blamed the PCJSS, which denied any part in it. In October the PCJSS and the UPDF exchanged fire for nearly two hours over territory near Sajek, where nearly 3,000 tourists had been taken out of the valley days earlier. In November the home minister said in a written reply that 316 people had been killed in attacks by armed groups in the hills in five years. That is the government's figure, given without a year-by-year account.

Behind the numbers

This story is our analysis of 50 years of violence in the hills, drawn from many kinds of sources that The Daily Star gathered and matched incident by incident: conflict datasets from UCDP and ACLED, the Political Violence in Bangladesh dataset, and reports by human rights investigators, commissions and journalists. Every number in it is a count of what someone wrote down. Where two of these sources cover the same years, each missed about half of the deadly incidents the other found, so the real toll is almost certainly higher.

Behind each of those numbers is a family like Sang Kung Bawam's, or Gunamala Chakma's, or Nurnahar Begum's.

The insurgency ended with the Accord. The land is still not settled, and the killing has not stopped. Nearly 29 years on, people in the hills still die: fewer at a time, by more hands, and more often in Bandarban.

In January this newspaper urged the parties to commit to the Accord in full, and warned that without stronger land governance, reformed local institutions, credible investigations and an end to impunity, "sustainable peace in the CHT will remain a distant dream".

How we did this

The data. We merged four datasets and six batches of news, rights-group and commission reports into 1,227 incidents, 1976 to 21 November 2026. 1,219 are in Rangamati, Khagrachhari and Bandarban or on their Myanmar border (43 of them placed only "in the hills"); 16 of those are arrests or seizures without violence, which no count in this story includes. Eight incidents just across the district line are kept apart. Reports of the same incident were matched by date, place and actors and merged once, with every source kept.

Each source in its own years. In this story, "the conflict researchers" are the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP), "the newspaper study" is the Political Violence in Bangladesh dataset, and "human rights investigators" are Amnesty International; ACLED is the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data project. Deadly incidents are compared over time only within one source, or the same two sources, inside the years they cover; attacks that killed ten or more are compared across all sources combined. A change at a coverage boundary is never read as a change in violence. UCDP includes violence only by a conflict or armed group that killed at least 25 people in a year, which is why the fighting between the hill parties never entered its count. Before 1989 there are only major events described afterwards, partly by parties to the conflict. The Political Violence in Bangladesh dataset changes its newspapers in 1999, thins in 2007–08 and adds 2018 from a separate release. ACLED's data run from 27 January 2010 to 27 May 2025, so coverage is fuller in those years, and a lower count since then reflects thinner coverage, not necessarily less violence. Some attacks this story describes from human rights reports, such as Langadu in May 1989 and Naniarchar in November 1993, did not come up in any of the datasets or reports behind the counts, so they are in no count or chart.

Deaths and estimates. Deaths are the middle of what the sources report, with the range where they disagree. "~" marks an estimate. A death toll stated only in a summary account, such as ~800 in one account of 1980, is never added to the count. Comparisons of the past decade are within one source; they show what that source recorded, and media coverage can change too. Every count is a minimum. From 27 January 2010 to the end of 2018, the Political Violence in Bangladesh dataset and ACLED each hold 107 deadly incidents in the hills on the merged figures (104 each on their own); 57 are in both and 157 in at least one, so each caught about half of the other's. That overlap suggests at least ~200 deadly incidents in those years (95% interval ~176–224). It is an illustrative floor, never a correction: both draw on the same newspapers, so the true number is probably higher. The rings in the charts show the tolls in the merged data, which can differ from the figures the accounts in the text give. Some rings rest on details we have not yet confirmed, and remain under review. Among them is the November 2000 border fighting, which rests on a single source, UCDP, that places it in Bandarban; we found no other account.

What this cannot show. The Accord ended the insurgency: the PCJSS signed it, and its fighters handed in their weapons. The numbers show when the other changes came, not why. The fighting had already slowed after the 1992 ceasefire, and a count of before and after cannot show that the Accord caused the smaller tolls, the shift south or the killings between hill parties. There are no rates per head. Every direction reported here was re-tested under nine alternative choices (other merges, low and high deaths, without disputed or partisan-only incidents, without border incidents); the ones in this story hold under all of them.
